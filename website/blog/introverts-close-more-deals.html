Networking psychology

Introverts close more conference deals than extroverts

2026-08-19 · 5 min read

You walked into Token2049, watched the loudest founder in the room work the bar for ninety minutes, and went home thinking you'd lost. Three weeks later his deal pipeline was empty and yours had two LOIs. Here's why that's not a fluke.

The conventional wisdom at crypto events — be louder, work the room, collect 200 cards — is wrong. It's been wrong since Adam Grant published his 2013 study on extraversion and negotiator performance. The data has been sitting there for over a decade, and the conference circuit still hasn't caught up.

The Grant study

In 2013, Adam Grant ran a series of experiments with 163 call-center employees and found that ambiverts — people who sit between introversion and extraversion — outsold both pure introverts and pure extroverts. The ambiverts pulled in 24% more revenue per hour than the loudest sellers and 32% more than the quietest.

The mechanism is listening. Ambiverts ask twice as many questions as extroverts in a sales conversation. They let the other person talk 60-70% of the time. They pick up on the buyer's actual concerns instead of steamrolling with their pitch.

At a crypto conference, this maps almost perfectly. The loudest person in the room is the one rehearsing their elevator pitch. The quietest person is the one asking the founder what they actually need.

Why the bar conversation fails

Grant's follow-up analysis showed that pure extroverts undershoot in one specific way: they mistake talking for selling. The louder they get, the less the other person reveals. By the end of the night the extrovert has had 30 conversations and learned nothing actionable in any of them.

Meanwhile the introvert who spends the same 90 minutes in three conversations has usually identified one buyer with a real problem. That's the one who gets a follow-up. That's the one who replies.

At a 500-person crypto event, three deep conversations outperform thirty shallow ones. The data has been saying this since 2013. The conference floor hasn't noticed.

The math is brutal. If you're the loud person at the bar, you walk away with 30 names and 0 signal. If you're the quiet person at the side table, you walk away with 3 names and 3 problems you can solve. Three months later the loud person is wondering why nobody replies. The quiet person is shipping a deal.

The three-question shortcut

You don't need to be an introvert to run this play. You need three questions and the discipline to shut up while the other person answers.

  • What are you actually building? — Not the pitch, not the tagline. The thing they'd tell a co-founder at 11pm.
  • What's the hardest part right now? — The real friction. The thing they can't easily fix.
  • Who else should I meet? — The connector question. This is the one that builds a network instead of a list.

Ask all three in the first 90 seconds of the conversation. Then listen. Do not pitch. Do not say what you do until they ask. The asymmetry is the whole trick.

What to capture
