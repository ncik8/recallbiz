Contact systems

Your conference contacts decay 22% a year. The four-field fix.

2026-09-04 · 5 min read

You walked out of Token2049 with 200 Telegram handles. Six months later you search the list for the founder you talked to at the L2 panel and you can't find her. The handle is there. The name is right. But there are three founders in your list with similar names, and you can't remember which one was on the panel — so you message the wrong person.

This is contact decay. And it doesn't just happen to you. ZoomInfo's 2025 research puts B2B contact decay at 22.5% per year across all industries — and up to 70% in fast-moving sectors like crypto. The data you captured at last year's event is already half-stale by next year's event.

The decay isn't random

The fields that decay fastest are the ones you don't think to capture in the moment. Job titles change. Companies rebrand. Telegram handles get abandoned. Roles shift. The handle you saved as @vitalik_dev in September belongs to a different person by March — or doesn't exist at all.

What survives is the specific, weird, contextual stuff. Not "CEO of X." Not "investor." The thing they said about ZK rollups. The booth number where they were standing when you met. The afterparty conversation that made you laugh. Those fragments don't decay — they get sharper with time, because they tie the contact to a memory your brain actually kept.

Decay isn't a data problem. It's a retrieval problem. You're not losing contacts — you're losing the ability to find them.

This is why the standard "save name, save company, save email" CRM approach fails at high-volume events. You're capturing the three fields most likely to be stale six months later, and skipping the three fields most likely to still be useful.

The four fields that survive

After running this system across four events, the four fields that hold up under retrieval pressure are:

  • Telegram handle — globally unique, DMable, doesn't decay the way titles do
  • One specific thing they said — a claim, a question, a disagreement, a quote
  • Where you met — booth, panel, afterparty, lunch table; the spatial anchor
  • What they're building or betting on — not their company, but their current obsession

Notice what's not on the list. Job title. Email address. Company name. Those decay. The four fields above decay slowly or not at all — because they're either immutable (handle), self-reinforcing (specific quote), or recoverable from context (location + obsession = who).

Why "search by handle" beats "search by name"

There are 50 Vitaliks at any crypto event. There is exactly one @vitalik_buterin_actual. The handle is the unique key. When you search by name, you're matching against fuzzy string equality and hoping for the best. When you search by handle, you're matching against a primary key.

This is why the Telegram-first contact system works. The handle isn't optional metadata — it's the index. Name, company, and role become attributes you attach to the handle, not the other way around.

The contact card of the future isn't sorted alphabetically by surname. It's sorted by signal — by the handles you've messaged most, by the people whose anchor fragments you can still recall, by the relationships that survived the season.

The 30-second capture loop

The reason you lose these four fields isn't laziness. It's friction. If saving a contact takes 30 seconds, you stop doing it after the tenth person at an event.

The system that works: capture in under 5 seconds. Forward the QR to a chat-native system, type the anchor phrase as a quick voice note, done. The four fields get indexed in seconds and become searchable for years. You don't write essays. You don't open a CRM. You forward a contact and say one weird thing about them.

Six months later when you're trying to find that panel founder, you don't search by name. You search by anchor — "ZK rollups are overhyped" — and the right Vitalik appears with the booth number and the afterparty context attached.

The compound interest on contacts

Conference contacts work like financial assets: they decay over time, but the ones you can still find compound. A 22% decay rate on a 200-contact list means 156 of them are still findable a year later — if you captured them right. If you captured them wrong, you're at 60, and most of those are the wrong 60.

The four-field capture isn't a productivity hack. It's a survival strategy. The conferences pay off three years later, when a 2026 Token2049 contact becomes your 2027 co-founder, your 2028 investor, your 2029 customer. That only works if you can still find them when it matters.
