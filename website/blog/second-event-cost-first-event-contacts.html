Networking Psychology

Why your second event costs you your first event's contacts

2026-08-01 · 6 min read

You walked out of Token2049 with 247 contacts and a head full of names. Six weeks later you fly into Devconnect. By the time the opening panel starts, half those Token2049 names are gone — not because you forgot them on purpose, but because your brain quietly replaced them with newer ones.

The cruel part: the contacts from the second event aren't even that good yet. But they're winning the memory contest anyway. Because of how memory works.

The interference nobody warns you about

Cognitive psychologists call it proactive interference — the phenomenon where newly learned information disrupts the retrieval of older, similar information. It was first formalised in 1925 by the British psychologist Lionel Standing, and it's been replicated hundreds of times since.

The pattern is brutal for conference attendees: the more conferences you attend in a season, the worse your recall of contacts from earlier events. Not because your memory is bad — because similar contexts compete with each other in retrieval.

A Token2049 panel discussion and a Devconnect panel discussion look identical to your hippocampus. A "founder from a ZK rollup company" at event one and a "founder from a ZK rollup company" at event two overwrite each other like two files saved to the same path. The most recent save wins.

The 48-hour spacing trap

The fix most people reach for is also the trap: attend events back-to-back and "keep up" by writing notes between sessions. But Anderson's 2003 review of spacing effects showed that distributed review only works when there's a meaningful delay — ideally more than a week.

Two events in the same week give you a worst case: too short a gap for the first event's contacts to consolidate into long-term memory, long enough for new similar contacts to start crowding them out.

The contact you remember best after a conference season is the one you used. Memory doesn't preserve contacts — it preserves retrievals.

This is why the people who reply within 24 hours and keep their network alive actually do remember their contacts. They didn't have better memory. They retrieved more often.

The encoding fix

Encoding is the only thing that defeats interference. Repeating a name to yourself at event two doesn't help — that's structural processing, the shallowest kind. Craik and Lockhart's 1972 levels-of-processing framework found that semantic encoding — tying a contact to a meaning, an opinion, a debate — survives 65% of the time versus 17% for surface-level repetition.

Concretely, this means your notes for each contact need at least one anchor that wouldn't fit anyone else. Job titles and company names fail the test (they collide constantly at crypto events). What survives:

  • Their stance on a specific debate ("thinks ZK rollups are overhyped")
  • A detail from their background that's memorable ("ex-trader turned LP, lost on FTX")
  • The exact question they asked you during the panel
  • Their reaction to your product or pitch ("said the dashboard was confusing")

Two contacts can share a job title, a company, a panel appearance, and a Telegram handle. They cannot share a stance on ZK rollups. That's the encoding layer that beats interference.

What to do between events

Three things make the difference. None of them are about working harder at the second event.

  • Touch every contact from event one before event two begins. Even a one-line Telegram message ("great meeting at Token2049 — did you ever look into the sequencer thing?") counts as a retrieval. Each retrieval deepens the trace.
  • Tag contacts by opinion, not by company. A database searchable by "overhyped ZK rollups" survives interference. A database searchable by "ZK rollup companies" does not.
  • Set the trip before the next one starts. /trip set Devconnect creates a clean container. New contacts go into a new bucket. Old contacts from Token2049 don't compete with new ones during retrieval.

The people who keep their network alive across a conference season aren't smarter. They just separated their buckets before the interference had a chance to do its work.
