Sales networking

Your best conference contact is rarely one person

trce · 2026-08-11 · 5 min read

You walked into Token2049 with one target in mind. By lunch, you have their Telegram handle, a half-finished conversation, and the familiar feeling that you should follow up before the room moves on.

That instinct is right, but the target is too small. In a serious B2B relationship, the person you met is rarely the whole opportunity. They are the thread into a team, a product, a budget, or a group chat you have not found yet.

The one-contact illusion

Conference networking trains you to collect individuals. You save a name, a company, and a role, then label the record “warm.” A month later, you send one message and wonder why the conversation stalls. The missing ingredient is not another reminder. It is the second and third person who make the first contact useful.

One contact can be enthusiastic and still unable to buy, approve, introduce, or explain what is happening internally. A connected set gives you context. It shows who owns the problem, who feels it, and who can move the next step.

A conference contact is not a relationship until you know what they connect you to.

The numbers behind the thread

HubSpot's 2025 sales statistics roundup cites Gong data showing that 77% of deals are multi-threaded. It also reports that successful deals involve twice as many buyer contacts as unsuccessful ones. This is not a reason to spray messages at everyone. It is a reason to stop treating one reply as the entire account.

The same roundup puts large strategic deals at an average of 17 contacts. Your conference list does not need 17 names for every opportunity, but it does need more than one when the problem is shared across product, partnerships, compliance, or finance.

Build the thread while the room is warm

The best time to find the next person is not after the first contact goes quiet. It is in the original conversation, while you still have a natural reason to ask. Do not ask for an org chart. Ask a question that makes the relationship easier to understand:

  • Who else is closest to this problem day to day?
  • Who usually joins the decision when this moves forward?
  • Which person at this event has the strongest opinion on it?
  • Would it be useful to compare notes with them together?

Those questions turn a handoff into a conversation. If the answer is a name you can meet at the same venue, make the introduction immediately. If it is someone remote, save the name with the reason they matter instead of trusting yourself to reconstruct the chain later.

Save the connection, not just the contact

Capture the first person with one sentence of context, then record the relationship around them: “knows partnerships lead,” “works with the compliance team,” or “introduced by the wallet infrastructure group.” Forward the handle to @trceiobot with that note before the next panel starts.

When you search six weeks later, you should be able to retrieve a path, not just a face. Start with the problem, the team, or the person they mentioned. The useful record is the one that tells you where to go next.

Turn one introduction into a real relationship map.

Save the contact, the context, and the connection while the conversation is still fresh.
