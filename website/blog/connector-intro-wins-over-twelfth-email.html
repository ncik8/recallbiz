Follow-up systems

Introducing two contacts is worth twelve follow-up emails

trce · 2026-08-07 · 6 min read

By the time you left Token2049, you had 200 contacts and a plan to email each one. Three weeks later, you've sent eleven "just bumping this up" messages, you've had two real conversations, and the rest of the list is a graveyard.

The plan was always wrong. Sending twelve "nice to meet you" notes is not networking. Networking is what happens when two people who didn't know each other end up talking because of you.

Givers finish first

Wharton professor Adam Grant spent a decade studying the three reciprocity styles in workplaces — giver, matcher, taker. Five years later, the givers in every group he tracked landed at the top of the performance distribution, not the bottom. The bottom belonged to the takers. The middle, the matchers, plateaued.

The givers didn't win by saying yes to every request. They won by spending five minutes a week doing something tactical: connecting two people they knew should know each other. Not asking. Connecting.

The highest-leverage move in your contact database isn't a follow-up — it's a forward.

Why connector favors beat pitch favors

There is a difference between asking and connecting. Asking burns your account balance with one person. Connecting deposits value with both of them at once.

  • Ask: you ask contact A for a warm intro. A spends social capital, you receive, A's balance with you drops.
  • Pitch: you send your deck to contact B. B's inbox now matches every other cold deck this week.
  • Connect: you write to A: "I'm meeting B next week, you two should swap notes on the same problem." Both of them get a useful intro. Your balance with both goes up.

HubSpot's 2024 State of Marketing report found that personalized one-to-one outreach (the kind that names a shared problem) earns reply rates around 22%, while generic deck-sends sit near 3%. A connector intro is a personalized one-to-one — for two people at once.

The 5-minute connector habit

After every event, open your contact list and ask a four-line question: who in here would benefit from meeting whom? Spend five minutes on this. Then write three connector messages that look like this:

  • "You both work on L2 sequencer decentralization — should I intro you?"
  • "B is raising for a stablecoin treasury, A runs OTC at a market maker — obvious pairing."
  • "Both of you mentioned the same custody pain point. Forwarding the chat."

Three intros is enough. That is fifteen minutes of writing that deposits value with six people, and turns you from "person who showed up at Token2049" into "person who makes introductions happen". People will remember that role long after they forget your company.

Where to keep the data so this is possible

This only works if your contact database is searchable by topic, not just by name. A spreadsheet with 200 names and no notes is a phonebook. A database with three context tags per person is a connector's tool.

That's why the next loop after an event is: tag each new contact by what they work on (a fund, a chain, a sector), not just who they are. The query "who in my list works on stablecoins" should take five seconds, not twenty minutes.
