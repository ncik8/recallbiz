Contact maintenance

Four numbers that prove your network is fake

trce · 2026-08-27 · 5 min read

You opened Telegram this morning and scrolled past 247 saved contacts. Then you sat there for ten seconds trying to remember who half of them were. By the thirtieth name you'd given up. That's not a network. That's a pile.

Most people at crypto conferences measure their network by total contacts saved. The number goes up. They feel rich. They screenshot it on New Year's Eve and post it as proof of hustle.

But the number is meaningless. A contact saved in a moment of enthusiasm is not a relationship. It's an artifact. Dunbar's research puts your real capacity at 150 stable connections and only about 15 in your inner circle. Everything else is decoration.

The four numbers that matter

Here's the audit. Run it on your Telegram, your LinkedIn, your phone. Four numbers. Less than five minutes total:

  1. Total contacts saved in your main address book.
  2. Contacts you've messaged in the last 90 days.
  3. Contacts who've messaged you back in the last 90 days.
  4. Contacts you'd actually call for advice on a Friday night.

That fourth number is the one that breaks people. "Who would I actually call?" turns out to be a much smaller list than the saved count. By the time the audit finishes, the answer is usually under 30. Sometimes under 15. Always less than you remember.

Numbers two and three are the ones that hurt. Most people who run this audit land somewhere embarrassing. Their "I sent them a message" rate is below 10%. Their "they wrote back" rate is below 5%.

Your network isn't the number you saved. It's the number who'd notice if you disappeared.

What good looks like

HubSpot's State of Marketing reports put cold outreach reply rates at 5-7% across B2B industries. Your warm network should hit 60-80% on a personal message, not 5. The gap between those numbers is what "real network" actually means.

If your third number — contacts who've messaged you back in the last 90 days — is above 15% of your total, you're in good shape. Below 5% and your list is mostly decoration. The math has nowhere to hide. Most of the 247 contacts sitting in your Telegram haven't sent you a single message in three months.

They aren't part of your network. They're an address book. The audit forces the question you usually avoid: what's the difference between an address book and a network? It's the same difference between a parking lot and a road. The parking lot holds cars. The road moves them.

Closing the gap

The fix is two parts.

First: stop saving contacts you wouldn't email back yourself. The three-second test is simple. Would I message this person first if they wrote to me? If no, skip the save. Most of your 247 are failing this test right now.

Second: send one low-effort message a month to people in your second-tier list. Not a pitch. A "saw this and thought of you" forward. Woodpecker's 2024 study showed 70% of sales outreach stops after one follow-up, so the bar for staying top-of-mind is impossibly low. The contact who hears from you once a quarter for two years is the one who picks up the phone in year three.
