Your first pitch loses to your second question

2026-08-05·5 min read

You walked into Token2049 with a tight 30-second pitch. You delivered it 47 times. You got 4 business cards back and 2 follow-ups three weeks later. By the time the closing party started, you realised the people you actually remembered weren't the ones you pitched — they were the two people who asked you something you couldn't answer.

The 30-second pitch is the default setting at every crypto event. It's also why your contact list is mostly people who won't reply.

What the research actually says

In 1988, Neil Rackham sat through 35,000 sales calls and published the findings in SPIN Selling. The pattern was unambiguous: top performers ask an average of 4.4 discovery questions before talking about their own product. Bottom performers ask 1.2. The discovery-first group closed deals at roughly twice the rate.

Gong's analysis of over a million B2B sales calls later confirmed the pattern in a different setting: the talk-to-listen ratio matters more than the script. Calls where the seller spoke less than 46% of the time had 2.1x higher win rates than calls where the seller spoke more than 55%.

The pitch doesn't win the contact. The question does. And the second question wins even harder than the first.

At a crypto conference, the same math applies. The intro where you delivered 30 seconds of talking got you remembered for what you do. The intro where you asked 3 questions got you remembered for what you cared about.

The 3-question stack

Most event intros go: name → role → "what do you do?" → answer → "cool, here's what I do." That's two monologues and zero real conversation.

Replace "what do you do?" with a 3-question stack. Each one is shorter than the last:

  • Situation: "What are you working on at {company} this quarter?" — surface what they're actually shipping.
  • Problem: "What's the hardest part of that right now?" — opens the door to a real answer.
  • Implication: "What happens if that doesn't get solved before {next event}?" — turns it into stakes.

By the time you get to the implication, you've spent maybe 90 seconds. They've told you three things they care about. You've shared almost nothing about yourself. And they're 4x more likely to remember the conversation than the person who pitched them.

Why this works at crypto events specifically

Crypto audiences are pitched 200 times a day. Founders, VCs, marketers, recruiters, journalists — everyone has the same 30 seconds about their protocol, fund, or service. The pitch is the noise floor.

The question is signal. When you ask "what's the hardest part of that right now?" the other person's brain stops scanning for the next conversation and lands. You're not another pitch. You're someone who actually wants to know.

The implication question is the one that creates a follow-up. Once someone has told you what happens if a problem doesn't get solved, your next message has a reason to exist. You're not "checking in." You're following up on the thing they said they care about.
