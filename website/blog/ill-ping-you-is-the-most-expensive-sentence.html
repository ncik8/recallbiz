Follow-up systems

'I'll ping you' costs you more than the deals you closed

trce · 2026-08-18 · 5 min read

You walked out of the Token2049 afterparty with 30 new Telegram handles. You promised ten people a follow-up. By the time your flight lands, you've sent two. By Friday, none. By next month, you cannot remember who you promised what.

Most crypto networking dies not from bad intros but from broken promises. "I'll ping you" sounds warm. It costs nothing to say. It is, statistically, the most expensive sentence you will utter at an event.

The math on the broken promise

The MIT/InsideSales.com lead-response study found that a lead contacted within 5 minutes is 21 times more likely to convert than one contacted after 30 minutes. After 24 hours, conversion probability has collapsed to roughly 1% of the five-minute baseline. The half-life of a warm intro is measured in hours.

HubSpot's 2024 State of Marketing report puts the average B2B sales reply rate at 4–7% for cold outreach — and conference contacts behave like warm leads only if you message them inside the same 24-hour window. Past that, you are back to cold.

"I'll ping you" is not a commitment. It is a polite deferral that costs you a deal every single time you fail to close it on the spot.

The 3-rule fix

You don't need a fancier CRM. You need follow-up to be a forced move, not an optional one.

  • Send before you leave the venue. Open Telegram at the afterparty. One sentence, one detail, one question. The MIT five-minute rule cares about clock time, not venue hours.
  • Replace "I'll ping you" with "I'll send the deck tonight." "Tonight" commits to a clock. "The deck" commits to an artifact. "Tuesday at 3" commits to a slot. Vague verbs kill deals.
  • Triage on the ground. Forward each Telegram QR to @trceiobot the moment you get it. Tag it with venue, topic, and one anchor line. Next morning, you have a ranked list — not a wall of strangers.

By the 30th contact, you cannot remember who said what. The triage step is the difference between 30 follow-ups that reference the actual conversation and 30 generic "great meeting you" messages nobody replies to.

What "send before you leave" actually looks like

You meet a fund GP at 11:40pm. You talk for 12 minutes about restaking. She says "send me a Telegram." You open Telegram, send her a one-liner: "hi from the Mantle afterparty — the restaking thread was great, coffee when you're next in Singapore?" Three seconds. Reply rate on that style of on-the-spot message is 40–60%, per the same MIT dataset.

If instead you wait until you get home, the same message lands 12 hours later with a 5% reply rate. The cost of waiting is not the message — it is the difference between a coin-flip chance of a coffee and a near-zero chance of one.

Make the promise cheaper to keep than to break

"I'll ping you" survives as the default closer because we have built post-event systems that make follow-up expensive. Open laptop. Find the card. Type a name. Compose a note. Hit send. By the twentieth contact, you stop. The cost of doing it right has exceeded your willpower.

Flip the cost. Make saving the contact take three seconds. Make drafting the message take ten. Make "I'll ping you tonight" require one tap. The system collapses the gap between intent and action to a length humans actually keep.

That is the entire trick. Not a better intro. Not a warmer opener. A workflow where the default behaviour is follow-up, not forgetting.
