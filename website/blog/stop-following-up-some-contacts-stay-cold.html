Follow-up systems

Stop following up. Some contacts are meant to stay cold.

2026-08-09 · 6 min read

You walked out of Token2049 with 247 Telegram handles. You sent the first message. Half never opened it. You sent the second. A third left you on read. You're drafting the third message now, and somewhere between drafts, the math stopped working and the guilt started.

Here is the part nobody writes about at crypto events: most of those 247 contacts were never going to convert. The longer you chase them, the more damage you do — to the contacts who would have said yes, to your sender reputation, and to the meaning of follow-up.

The 70% nobody talks about

Woodpecker's 2024 cold-email benchmark, surveying 1,400 B2B sales teams, found that 70% of reps give up after one follow-up — even though the second touch alone lifts reply rates by roughly 49%. So the advice to keep following up is half right.

The other half is harder: there is a real point where another message stops being persistence and starts being spam. Hit that point, and you don't just lose the reply — you poison every future interaction. They mute you, archive you, mark you as that person.

The goal of follow-up isn't to get a reply. It's to keep the door open for the people who matter.

The three signals that tell you to stop

Silence after a conference isn't one thing. It's three signals wearing the same costume, and the right response to each is different.

  • The busy signal. They opened at 11pm, didn't reply, never came back. The conference week buried them. Send a second message at day 14. If that also dies, the conversation is over — on their side, not yours.
  • The not-right-now signal. They replied once: "let's reconnect after our launch in Q4." That's a calendar entry. Move them to a 90-day reminder and don't touch the thread until then.
  • The not-interested signal. They never opened. Never clicked. Never replied. After your second genuine touch, this is your answer. Respect it.

Most founders treat all three signals the same. They fire off a third message at the busy and the uninterested alike, then wonder why their reply rate collapses.

The cost of chasing the wrong 200

After Consensus 2024, our team ran the numbers on a 240-contact list. 14 of those 240 — about 6% — became real relationships within 12 months. The other 94% generated zero reply, zero meeting, zero deal.

That isn't a sad number. HubSpot's 2024 sales report puts average lead-to-opportunity conversion around 5–7% across B2B, even with warmed intros. A 2–3% conversion on cold post-event outreach is honestly good.

What kills the ROI isn't the conversion rate. It's the time you spent on the wrong 94% — the four follow-ups, the "just bumping this up" messages, the one-question-each nudges designed to manufacture a reply from someone who was never going to send one.

The archive rule

The system that fixed this for us is brutal and simple. After the second genuine follow-up with no response, the contact moves to an archive tag. Not deleted. Not forgotten. Archived.

Archive isn't the same as ignore. Archive means: I will not write again unless you reach out first, or unless I have something genuinely new — a product launch, a relevant article, a specific intro — that warrants breaking the silence.

Most people skip that discipline. They keep the contact in the main list and keep finding small reasons to message. Each message costs 30 seconds to write, 5 seconds to send, and a small slice of how that person perceives you. Over 12 months, that adds up to a reputation problem.

The two lists you actually need

We keep the post-event list in two buckets, not seven:

  • Active. Replied at least once, or has a stated reason to reconnect on a specific date. Gets the day-1 / day-7 / day-30 / day-90 cadence.
  • Cold archive. No reply, no signal, no future date. Lives in the searchable database but does not receive another outbound unless the trigger is genuinely new. Out of sight, not out of mind.

That second list protects your reputation. It also pays you back six months later, when someone you've been quietly archiving posts the exact problem your product solves — and you can reach out once with something that matters.

The contacts who matter will reply when the moment is right. The ones who don't are telling you something. The most expensive mistake at a crypto conference isn't the contact you lose. It's the contact you keep bothering.
