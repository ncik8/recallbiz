How to find a contact when you only remember a fragment

2026-08-06·5 min read

You walked into the office on Tuesday with a name you couldn't place. A guest you mentioned on a podcast last week. A VC you met at a side event. You remember they were at the L2 sequencer panel. You remember they used a phrase about "intents, not transactions." You remember almost nothing else. By the time you scroll past the 14th row of names in your contact list, you've given up.

This is the second failure of conference networking. The first is forgetting the name. The second is having 600 contacts saved and no way to find the one you actually need.

The visual scan breaks at 200

If you meet 30 people at each event and attend six events a year, you cross 200 active contacts in roughly fourteen months. The CRM contact-card view — first name, last name, company, role — works at 30. It collapses at 200 because the retrieval cue you have is almost never the cue the column is sorted by.

You don't remember the surname. You remember they were wearing a green jacket. You remember they mentioned an airdrop thesis. You remember they disagreed with the restaking pitch. None of that is searchable in a CRM card view.

A 2024 review in ACM Computing Surveys put it as a "vocabulary mismatch" problem: the words you used to capture the contact rarely match the words you later use to retrieve it. The fix is a different retrieval interface.

You don't need to remember more about the contact. You need a way to find them with what you already remember.

The fragment-search prompt

Semantic search — search by meaning, not by exact match — changes what you can ask for. Instead of "show me contacts at company X" you can ask "who at Token2049 disagreed with the restaking pitch?" The retrieval engine parses your question, scans every note you've ever written about that contact, and returns the closest match.

The prompts that work aren't full sentences. They're fragments:

  • The phrase cue: "intents not transactions" — finds anyone you saved with that exact phrase.
  • The context cue: "Devconnect afterparty, sequencer panel" — narrows by event and sub-context.
  • The opinion cue: "said restaking was overhyped" — finds the contact by the claim they made.
  • The visual cue: "green jacket, white cap" — works if your contact notes recorded a distinguishing detail.

Each of these is a one-line prompt. The retrieval system handles the matching. You don't have to remember the name, the company, or the role — just any one detail from the conversation.

The capture side has to keep up

Fragment search only works if your capture side records fragments. A contact card with just a name and a Telegram handle is invisible to semantic search. The capture has to capture the texture of the conversation, not just the metadata.

The minimum fragment set per contact, the one that makes fragment search actually return useful results:

  • One phrase they actually said — verbatim, in quotes, in their voice.
  • One claim they made — a thesis, a prediction, a stance on a contested topic.
  • One distinguishing detail — what they were wearing, where you met them, who introduced you.
  • One next step — the thing you agreed to send, the conversation you agreed to continue.

When all four are saved, fragment search has four independent cues to match against. Even if three are gone by month six, the fourth usually finds them.

The system

The fragment-search habit only matters when your contact list outgrows what you can scan by eye. That's the point most networkers hit around the second conference season. By then you're adding friction, not contacts.

Forward the Telegram QR with the four fragments inline — the phrase, the claim, the detail, the next step. Search by any of them six months later. The contact you forgot you had is the one you needed.
