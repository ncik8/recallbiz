The follow-up after a crypto event that gets a reply

trce · 2026-07-28 · 4 min read

You walked into KBW on Wednesday. By Friday you had 180 Telegram QRs in your chat and 240 paper cards in a stack. You sat down on Saturday to "send follow-ups." Three hours later, you'd sent 14 messages, all of them basically the same: Great meeting you at [event], let's stay in touch.

Of those 14, you got 3 replies. By Monday, two of those threads had died. The 240 cards are still in a pile.

This is what the data predicts. A 2024 HubSpot survey of sales activity found that 48% of salespeople never make a single follow-up attempt. Of the 52% who do, the vast majority send something generic. Belkins's cold-email benchmark reports that the first follow-up can lift reply rates by 49% — but only if it adds something the first message didn't.

Why "great meeting you" vanishes

The "great meeting you" message is the conference equivalent of a subject line that says "Quick question." It's not wrong. It's just indistinguishable from the 200 other intros the recipient got this week. Your contact opens Telegram, sees your message, sees 14 messages from other people who all said the same thing, and closes the chat.

The follow-up doesn't fail because the person wasn't interested. It fails because the message gave them no reason to respond instead of ignoring.

The hard part isn't sending a follow-up. The hard part is sending one that doesn't sound like everyone else's. That requires a specific kind of context — and you need to capture it before you leave the event.

The five elements of a reply-getting follow-up

After watching what works across hundreds of post-event follow-ups, the messages that get replies share five things. None of them are "be more personal."

  • A specific reference. "We talked about your L2 sequencer decentralisation bet" beats "we had a great chat" every time.
  • A shared artefact. A paper they cited, a tweet they sent, a panel they were on, a protocol they launched. The follow-up proves you listened.
  • A new piece of information. A link, a number, a thought that wasn't in the original conversation. Mailshake data shows only 5% of senders personalize every message; the ones who add something new are the ones who don't get archived.
  • One clear next step. A 15-minute call, a DM thread, a shared doc — not "let's find time."
  • Short length. Four sentences. Anything longer gets read on desktop next week.

If your follow-up has three of these five, you'll get a reply. If it has one, it goes in the read pile with the other 14.

Capture the context at the event, not after

The reason most follow-ups fail isn't that the sender didn't care. It's that they waited. By Sunday, the "specific reference" is gone. The name of the protocol they mentioned is gone. The fact that they were at the Sequoia afterparty is gone. You remember the person was important. You don't remember why.

The fix is to capture the anchor while it's still vivid — the disagreement, the question they asked, the panel they were sitting next to. The first follow-up isn't where you decide whether the contact survives. The capture step is. The follow-up is just where you find out.
