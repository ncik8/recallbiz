Conference tactics

The 72-hour conference protocol that decides if the trip pays off

trce · 2026-08-14 · 7 min read

By the time your flight to Singapore touches down, half the value of the trip is already locked in. The other half will be decided before you unpack. The MIT/InsideSales Lead Response Management study found that leads contacted within five minutes are 21 times more likely to convert than those contacted after 30 minutes. Most conference networking inverts that curve on purpose: we meet people, fly home, sleep for a day, then remember to follow up on Wednesday.

Three windows decide whether the trip paid off: the 24 hours before, the 48 hours on the ground, and the 24 hours after. Miss any of them and the contact decays into a Telegram handle you'll never message.

Window 1: The 24 hours before you fly

Pre-event research is the cheapest leverage you have. The attendee list is usually public two weeks out. The speaker list always is. The side-event calendar lives in three different Telegram groups by day ten.

Pick the 20 people you want to meet. Not 200 — twenty. For each, write one sentence on why this conversation is worth having. Then look up the side event where at least five of them will be.

The goal of pre-event research is not to learn about people. It is to walk into the venue already knowing three names in the room.

A short list beats a long list because you'll actually remember it. Twenty names, one sentence each, fits on a single screenshot. That's the deck you scroll through while waiting at the gate.

Window 2: The 48 hours on the ground

Once you're there, the only metric that matters is capture latency. How many seconds between meeting someone and saving them?

InsideSales.com's research on lead response time shows contact within five minutes converts at 21x the rate of contact after 30 minutes. The same math applies to conference contacts: a Telegram QR forwarded to @trceiobot while you're still mid-conversation preserves details you'd forget by the next booth.

Concrete capture protocol:

  • Forward every QR to the bot within five seconds of receiving it
  • Glance at the contact card before saving — confirm the spelling of their name
  • Tag the side event, the panel, the booth. Tagging is the index you'll search later
  • One anchor sentence: what they claimed, asked, or disagreed with

The anchor is the part most people skip. It's also the only thing that lets you write a follow-up message three weeks later that doesn't sound like a template.

Window 3: The 24 hours after

HubSpot's sales benchmark data shows response rates fall off a cliff after the first day. Contact within 24 hours sits in the 40-60% reply band. Wait a week and you're at 10-15%.

Two-day conferences produce 100-250 contacts. Hand-writing 200 follow-ups takes about four hours and produces 30 useful messages. The math doesn't work unless you batch.

The trick is to draft in bulk the morning after, send in two waves the day after that, and stop. Two messages beats five. Five messages flags you as spam. HubSpot's 2024 sales enablement report found that most B2B reply rates peak at the second touch and decay on the third.

A good follow-up is three sentences:

  • The anchor from the conversation ("the ZK rollup take you had at the sequencer panel")
  • The specific next step you proposed ("send the restaking thread on Tuesday")
  • The bare ask ("are you around for coffee Wednesday at the afterparty")

The compound effect

One conference with a working 72-hour protocol produces roughly 150 retrievable contacts and 30 warm replies. Five conferences a year with the same protocol is 750 contacts and a network that compounds. Most attendees do three conferences a year and walk away with 30 forgotten handles, then blame the events.

The events weren't the problem. The protocol was missing.
